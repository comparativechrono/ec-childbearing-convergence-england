# Methods

*Version 2 (October 2026). Changes from version 1 are listed in `CHANGELOG.md`.*

## Study design and setting

We conducted a population-level descriptive and projection study for England, quantifying the demographic convergence between the age at which women are diagnosed with uterine (predominantly endometrial) cancer and the age at which women give birth. The analysis uses routinely collected, publicly available, anonymised aggregate data and involves no individual patient contact. England was chosen so that cancer registration (national, via the National Disease Registration Service) and the cancer denominators align; for the long-run childbearing trend, where England-only series are not published historically, England and Wales data were used and validated against England-only figures for the most recent year (see "Geography and validation").

## Terminology

"Uterine cancer" denotes registrations coded to ICD-10 C54 (malignant neoplasm of the corpus uteri) or C55 (malignant neoplasm of uterus, part unspecified), the official NDRS uterine grouping. It includes endometrial carcinomas of all histological types and the much rarer uterine sarcomas. "Endometrial cancer" is used for the clinical disease and for the NDRS "Uterus – endometrial" group. Because 96.6% of uterine cancer registrations in 2023 were in the endometrial group, uterine cancer is used as the population-level proxy for endometrial cancer in all age-resolved analyses.

## Data sources

**Uterine cancer incidence by age.** Female cancer registrations for England, 2001–2023, were obtained from the National Disease Registration Service (NDRS) CancerData incidence tool, disaggregated by single diagnosis year, sex, five-year age band, and three-character ICD-10 site. The primary disease definition was ICD-10 **C54–C55**; corpus uteri alone (C54) was analysed as a sensitivity analysis. Counts by age band reconcile exactly to the published all-ages totals (for example, 9,048 female C54–C55 registrations in 2023). The incidence-trend figure uses the NDRS three-year rolling age-standardised rates, which smooth year-to-year variation, whereas all age-resolved analyses use single-year registrations, which are complete for every year. The export gives no age-standardised rate for the single year 2012 or for the 2012–2014 rolling window (the counts are present), so the incidence-trend figure shows 2012–2014 as a gap rather than drawing a line across it, and the all-ages APC (below) omits 2012.

**Endometrial group and stage.** Because the age-banded export is provided for all stages combined, the composition by NDRS group and the stage distribution were taken from the NDRS machine-readable cancer-registration tables for 2023 (Table 1, incidence by NDRS group), which separates "Uterus – endometrial" from "Uterus – non-endometrial" and gives stage at diagnosis. NDRS assigns these groups from tumour morphology; the endometrial group comprises endometrial carcinomas of all histological types (not only endometrioid), and the non-endometrial group (3.4%) is consistent in size with the approximately 3% of uterine cancers that are sarcomas. Stage is as registered by NDRS, using the TNM 8th edition (which, for carcinoma of the corpus uteri, corresponds to FIGO 2009 staging), the version applied to diagnoses up to 31 December 2025. These data are all-ages.

**Childbearing.** Birth data were taken from the Office for National Statistics (ONS) "Births in England and Wales: birth registrations" workbook (2025 edition). Four tables were used: live births by mother's area of usual residence and age (Table 2); maternities by country, place of birth, number of previous live-born children, and mother's age (Table 4); live births and age-specific fertility rates by country and parent age, 1938–2025 (Table 10); and mothers' standardised mean age by true birth order, 1938–2025 (Table 11). The **first-birth distribution** was operationalised as maternities with zero previous live-born children (Table 4), which is the closest published proxy for births to women who have not previously given birth; we note this counts maternities rather than live births and therefore treats a multiple first birth as one event.

**Forward projection.** Projected births by single year of mother's age for English local authorities, 2023–2047, were taken from the ONS 2022-based subnational population projections (births by age of mother, all babies ("persons"); migration category variant, which ONS recommends in place of the principal projection). English local authorities (area codes beginning "E") were summed to England totals for each projection year. The projections allocate all births to maternal ages 15–44 (the single-age figures sum exactly to the all-ages total), so in the projection "40 and over" refers to the 40–44 band.

## Harmonisation of age scales

The cancer and childbearing distributions were placed on a common set of five-year age bands spanning under-15 to 90-and-over. Cancer's sub-15 bands were collapsed to a single "<15" category; ONS "Under 20" was treated as 15–19 and "50 and over" as 50–54 (births above 50 are negligible). Table 10, which provides the year-by-year back-series, publishes mothers' ages as under 20, five-year bands to 35–39, and **"40 and over"**; births at 40 and over were assigned to the 40–44 band (births at 45 and over are under 1% of all births). Each distribution was normalised to sum to one over its full reported support before any comparison.

## Overlap coefficient

For each comparison we computed the overlap coefficient between the age density of cancer diagnoses, *f*c(a), and the age density of births, *f*b(a), defined on the shared harmonised bands as the sum over bands of the band-wise minimum, Σ min(*f*c, *f*b). This is the standard distributional overlap measure (range 0–1; 0 = disjoint, 1 = identical) and is the metric used in the analogous breast-cancer "collision" analysis. The headline comparison was the 2023 cancer distribution against the 2025 first-birth distribution (both England). The temporal trend, 2001–2023, used the all-births age distribution available for every year (England and Wales, Table 10) as the childbearing comparator, because a first-birth age distribution is not published England-only for the full back-series; a second series held the 2025 first-birth distribution fixed so that only the cancer side varied.

**Interpretation.** In every band below age 45 a smaller percentage of all cancers than of all births falls in the band, so the band-wise minimum is the cancer percentage; from 45 upwards the minimum is the birth percentage. The coefficient is therefore, in practice, the share of cancers diagnosed at ages 15–44 plus the small share of births at 45 and over. For 2023 against 2025 first births this is 0.0439 + 0.0041 = 0.048. This is why the coefficient responds to changes in the age at which cancers are diagnosed but hardly at all to further postponement of births.

**Overlap figure.** Each distribution is drawn as its band proportions joined at the band midpoints. Ages are in completed years, so the midpoint of 15–19 is 17.5. The births curve starts at the 15–19 band, which holds the ONS "Under 20" group, because there is no separate under-15 group. The shaded area is the region beneath both curves, that is, the pointwise minimum of the two lines evaluated on a fine age grid. It therefore includes the small triangle where the curves cross between the 40–44 and 45–49 midpoints. The shading illustrates the overlap; the coefficient itself is the band sum defined above. Panel b enlarges ages 20–55, where the two distributions meet.

## Reproductive-age burden and pace of change

As assumption-free complements to the overlap coefficient, we computed the annual number and proportion of female C54–C55 registrations occurring below ages 40, 45 and 50, counted from age 15 (that is, ages 15–39, 15–44 and 15–49; the six registrations under 15, two in each of 2001, 2016 and 2023, were excluded), and the 90th percentile of the first-birth age distribution (by linear interpolation within bands), interpreted as the upper edge of contemporary first-time childbearing.

The pace of change, 2001–2023, was summarised as the annual percentage change (APC), estimated by log-linear quasi-Poisson regression of annual registrations on calendar year, without an offset (counts) and with the log of the female population in the same age range as offset (rates). 95% confidence intervals use the Pearson-based dispersion parameter (floored at 1). Female population denominators were recovered from the NDRS all-cancer age-specific rates (count × 100,000 / rate; NDRS rates use ONS mid-year population estimates). These reproduce the published uterine-cancer age-specific rates to within 0.05 per 100,000. The 2012 rates are blank in the export, so the 2012 population was interpolated geometrically between 2011 and 2013. For comparison, the APC of the all-ages age-standardised rate was estimated by least-squares regression of the log rate on year (single-year rates; 2012 unavailable).

## Forward projection metrics

Holding the cancer diagnosis-age distribution fixed at 2023, the overlap coefficient equals the share of cancers diagnosed at 15–44 plus the share of births at 45 and over (see "Interpretation"). Since the ONS projection places no births at 45 and over, the projected coefficient is identical in every year (0.044), and this insensitivity is reported explicitly. Accordingly, the projection is summarised through directly interpretable demographic quantities: the mean age of mother, and the share and absolute number of births occurring at ages 35 and over and 40 and over. These measure the growth of the childbearing population entering the age range in which endometrial cancer begins to be diagnosed.

## Geography and validation

Cancer data are England-only throughout. Current-year childbearing distributions (first births and all births, 2025) are England-only (Tables 2 and 4, area of usual residence). The longitudinal childbearing comparator (Table 10) and the long mean-age series (Table 11) are England and Wales. To justify using England and Wales as a proxy for England in those series, we compared the standardised mean age at first birth for England against England and Wales in 2025, where both are published; the difference was 0.0 years, indicating that Wales (about 5% of births) does not materially shift the maternal-age distribution.

## Sensitivity analysis

All overlap and age-group statistics were recomputed for corpus uteri only (C54), excluding registrations of uterus, part unspecified (C55). The C55 share of registrations fell from 5.6% in 2001 to 1.5% in 2023.

## Software and reproducibility

All analyses were performed in Python 3 using pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, SciPy, and openpyxl. The complete pipeline runs from the raw downloaded files to every figure and reported statistic without manual intervention; the accompanying notebook reproduces the entire analysis and writes all outputs to a single directory.

## Limitations

The age-resolved cancer data are for all stages and all uterine histologies combined. The fertility-sparing-eligible subgroup (grade 1 endometrioid carcinoma confined to the endometrium: FIGO 2009 stage IA without myometrial invasion, FIGO 2023 stage IA1) could not be identified; grade and myometrial invasion are not reported, and stage is available only in aggregate (all-ages) form. The overlap is therefore computed on all uterine cancer and represents an upper envelope of the strictly eligible population. Registry data also lack molecular classification (POLE-mutant, mismatch-repair deficient, no specific molecular profile, p53-abnormal), which now informs both risk stratification and the suitability of fertility-sparing treatment. The older histology-based type I/II distinction cannot be reconstructed either. C55 registrations are of unspecified uterine origin (sensitivity analysis above). The first-birth measure is a maternities-based proxy. The longitudinal childbearing comparator is England and Wales rather than England-only (validated as above), and its oldest published age group is open-ended (40 and over). Five-year banding limits resolution at the tails. Finally, the convergence quantified here is demographic, not aetiological: the analysis does not assert that delayed childbearing causes endometrial cancer (later age at last birth is in fact protective), but characterises the growing overlap between the populations affected by each, which is what determines clinical demand for uterus-conserving treatment.
